# AI Slop is Obvious

# How to Spot AI-Written Text: Tells, Limits, and Why It Matters

I recently heard someone say they were using ChatGPT to write a condolence card to their best friend whose child had died. My first thought was: you are probably your best friend’s worst friend. The message will feel insincere, and at this point LLM writing is often so obvious that you may as well be smacking them with a sock full of pennies and shouting that you feel nothing about their loss.

But then I realized that most people probably won’t spot it right away. I can because I’ve spent hundreds of hours reading logs between AI chatbots and the people they’ve harmed or killed. I’ve developed something like a radar for LLM speech. This guide is for that grieving friend, and for anyone who wants to know why their boyfriend suddenly knows how to type full sentences and use an em dash after being called out, or why a book sounds like it was written by a deranged toaster.

To illustrate that “deranged toaster” voice, I’ll partly quote from *Shy Girl*, a book that was recently pulled from shelves after accusations of AI writing. The author later said an anonymous friend may have used AI to edit the book, and that she did not read or notice the changes after getting the “edited” manuscript back. Its first edition also used cover art taken from artist Whyn Lewis without permission. The author said she found the image on Pinterest and did not expect the book to reach a large audience, which is not reassuring. *Shy Girl* became a perfect collection of AI-sounding tells, and those tells are what started the wave of accusations that got the book removed from shelves.

There is a weird feeling to AI prose. It is technically correct but fundamentally wrong, like hearing someone put adjectives in the wrong order. Most native English speakers know it should be “big green plastic hat,” not “plastic green big hat.” Once you’ve engaged with LLM writing enough, you develop a similar spidey-sense. Here are the main tells.

## 1. Em dash overuse

AI uses em dashes far more than most humans do, and it often uses them where a comma, colon, parentheses, or period would work better. The dash is meant to mark an abrupt thought shift or an explanatory break, but AI uses it formulaically, as if it is constantly trying to create drama and suspense.

Example:  
*“Then I remembered—the employee group chat where all of our numbers were listed, laid bare for everyone to see whether we liked it or not.”*

That em dash is doing the work of a comma. One em dash is not a tell by itself. The tell is overuse, especially in places where a comma or colon would fit better. This tell has become so well known that newer AI models have started suppressing em dashes to hide it, so it may become less useful over time.

## 2. Negative parallelism

AI loves a structure called negative parallelism, which comes in two common patterns:

- **“Not just X, but Y.”**  
  Example: *“Her lipstick is red—not just red, but vivid, the kind of color that demands attention and gets it.”*

- **“It’s not X, it’s Y.”**  
  Example: *“It’s not romance—it’s curiosity.”*

AI uses this because it creates contrast and feels declarative. But it often sounds like it is correcting a misconception nobody had. For example: *“It’s not just an anniversary—it’s a commemoration.”* An anniversary is a commemoration. Humans rarely repeat this structure over and over, but AI may use it multiple times per paragraph because it feels persuasive.

## 3. Everything in threes

AI has a fixation on listing things in threes: three adjectives, three nouns, or three short phrases. Three feels comprehensive without requiring actual comprehensiveness.

The real giveaway is when the three items do not need to be three. AI might describe something as *“elegant, refined, and sophisticated”* or say a company focuses on *“advancement, progress, and development.”* In context, those words are near synonyms and don’t add meaning. If the three items are functional—*big, orange, round*—that’s fine. AI often uses threes to make shallow analysis look deeper.

## 4. Similes that don’t make sense

AI sometimes produces similes that sound meaningful but fall apart under the slightest scrutiny. *Shy Girl* contains several:

- *“Just a piece of paper slapped against the door, tilted at an angle like it’s mocking me.”* How is that mocking?
- *“Each second marked like the tick of a clock.”* That’s not like the tick of a clock. That is the tick of a clock.
- *“I feel like I’ve stepped outside myself, like I’m preparing a mannequin for something it won’t remember.”*

A simile is supposed to make something more relatable by comparing it to common experience. These comparisons don’t clarify anything; they just sound vaguely literary.

## 5. Overused “AI vocabulary”

AI tends to reuse certain fancy words: *delve, intricate, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, showcase, garner, crucial, enhance*. One “pivotal” could be a coincidence. A cluster of these words in a short piece is much more suspicious. This tell changes over time as models are updated, but the broader pattern is the same: AI reaches for these words constantly, while most humans use them only occasionally.

## 6. “Look how famous” phrasing

AI often overstates how famous or important someone is by focusing on media coverage and public recognition. Instead of explaining what someone did, it writes things like *“has received widespread acclaim,” “has been featured in major publications,”* or *“has garnered significant attention.”*

Humans usually don’t write this way. If someone is actually important, you just say what they did and the facts speak for themselves. AI adds a layer of “look how much attention this got” because it tracks notability through coverage metrics rather than actual impact. Humans focus on reality; AI focuses on coverage of reality because that’s all it has.

## 7. Corporate brandspeak

AI often writes like a marketing department. Even when you ask for neutral or encyclopedic prose, it defaults to words like *vibrant, nestled, groundbreaking, renowned,* and *showcasing*. It acts as if every topic needs to be sold. For products, it generates flowery descriptions like *sleek silhouette* and *bold proportions*. Newer AI is subtler, but it still has an underlying tone of “please think this is important and special.” If writing sounds like it is trying to convince you a mundane topic is worth caring about instead of just describing it, that’s a tell.

## 8. Vague attribution

AI frequently uses weasel wording: *experts contend, critics maintain, scholars have identified, sources indicate, research shows, it has been noted that*. These phrases sound authoritative but don’t tell you who is making the claim or what evidence exists. That’s the point—AI is hedging without backing anything up, partly because it may be hallucinating the sources anyway.

AI also overgeneralizes. It may cite one or two sources and then imply the view is widely held, or mention “modern researchers” and “scholars” without naming anyone.

## 9. Too many transitions

AI has a limited toolkit of transition words and uses them constantly: *on the other hand, moreover, in addition, furthermore, however, in contrast*. These show up everywhere, sometimes multiple times in a single paragraph. Often the transition doesn’t even make sense:

- *“The restaurant serves Italian food. Moreover, it offers a fabulous wine selection.”*
- *“The region receives significant rainfall. However, agriculture remains a major industry.”*

Real writers use transitions sparingly and naturally. AI uses them like a checklist, linking every sentence whether or not it needs to be linked.

## Can we really tell?

Most untrained people are not good at identifying AI writing. Some research has found that general human scoring can be very inconsistent. But people who have spent a lot of time interacting with ChatGPT and similar models can become highly accurate detectors without additional training, especially when several experienced people compare judgments.

One thing you should not rely on is AI detection tools. Websites and apps like GPTZero and Originality.AI claim to scan text and provide a percentage confidence score, but they are really just pattern-matching guesses. They contradict each other constantly. A human can write formally and hit AI patterns; an AI can be prompted to write casually and avoid them. Detection tools don’t know anything. Use your own brain first, and never accuse someone based solely on a random website.

## You don’t need certainty to reject slop

You will almost never know for certain whether something was AI-written unless it was published before LLMs existed. You could see every tell, run it through five detectors, and still not have absolute proof. People can write badly, overuse transitions, or sound generic on their own.

That lack of certainty doesn’t have to be frustrating. You can choose not to engage with slop. If something reads like it was generated by a machine that has never known the caress of a lover or how it feels to be stung by a hateful bee, you are allowed to simply not read it. You don’t need proof to decide that it feels like a waste of your time.

## Why it matters

The ability to distinguish real information from generated nonsense is a critical thinking skill. If you can’t tell whether something is real or fake, you are vulnerable to being fed whatever someone wants you to believe.

There are also larger reasons to care. AI data centres require significant water and cooling infrastructure, with growing concerns about effects on local ecosystems and communities. Servers become obsolete quickly, creating electronic waste that contains toxic materials, while manufacturing and cooling systems add further environmental damage. These costs are often far from the people using the technology.

AI is also trained on human writing without consent or payment. Every phrase, structure, and transition pattern was learned from millions of hours of human work that the companies never paid for or asked permission to use. When a human writes something wrong or harmful, there is a person to hold responsible. When AI writes it, blame can be shrugged off as “the algorithm” or “the training data.” That unaccountability is useful for bad actors.

Mediocrity matters too. When a new chat model pushes shallow, formulaic writing into millions of contexts at once, it threatens to make that mediocrity the baseline. We do not have to accept that.

You don’t need to become a content cop. You just need to keep your critical thinking cap on, trust your own bullshit detector, and remember that you always have the option to close the tab.
